\chapter[Inner Product Spaces]{Inner Product Spaces and Spectral Theory}\label{chap:inner-products-spectral-theory}

Chapter~6 asks when an operator has an eigenbasis, and Chapter~7 classifies
arbitrary operators algebraically. An inner product supplies extra geometry:
length, angle, and orthogonality. The new question is therefore stronger:
when can an eigenbasis be chosen orthonormally? In finite dimensions the
answer leads to the spectral theorem and to singular value decomposition,
which describes even maps that are neither normal nor endomorphisms.

Throughout this chapter \(V,W\) are finite-dimensional real or complex vector
spaces. We use the convention that a complex inner product is linear in its
first variable and conjugate-linear in its second.

\section{Inner Products and Norms}
Length and angle in \(\mathbb R^n\) come from the dot product. The following
axioms preserve exactly the algebraic features of that example, including the
complex conjugation needed to make \(\langle v,v\rangle\) nonnegative.

\begin{definition}
An \emph{inner product} on \(V\) is a map \(\langle\ ,\ \rangle:V\times V
\to F\), where \(F=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), such that for all
\(u,v,w\in V\) and \(a,b\in F\),
\[
\langle au+bv,w\rangle=a\langle u,w\rangle+b\langle v,w\rangle,
\qquad \langle v,u\rangle=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle},
\]
and \(\langle v,v\rangle\geq0\), with equality only for \(v=0\).
The induced \emph{norm} is \(\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}\).
\end{definition}

On \(F^n\), the standard inner product is
\[
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_i u_i\overline{v_i}.
\]
On matrix space \(M_{m,n}(F)\), the formula
\(\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(AB^*)\) is another useful example,
where \(B^*=\overline{B}^{\,t}\) and \(\operatorname{tr}\) is the sum of the
diagonal entries.

Conjugate symmetry converts first-variable linearity into
\(\langle u,av+bw\rangle=\overline a\langle u,v\rangle+
\overline b\langle u,w\rangle\). In particular,
\(\|av\|=|a|\|v\|\). These elementary consequences explain both the
placement of conjugates in the complex formulas and the use of an ordinary
positive square root in the norm.

\begin{example}[Why conjugation is necessary]
The apparently simpler formula
\[
\sum_i u_iv_i
\]
is not an inner product on \(\mathbb C^n\): for \(u=(1,i)\), it gives
\(1+i^2=0\), although
\(u\ne0\). By contrast, the standard formula gives
\(\langle u,u\rangle=|1|^2+|i|^2=2\). Conjugation is exactly what turns
the complex dot product into a genuine squared length.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz and triangle inequality]
For \(u,v\in V\),
\[|\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\,\|v\|,\qquad
\|u+v\|\leq\|u\|+\|v\|.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If \(v=0\), the first inequality is clear. Otherwise positivity of
\(\|u-\langle u,v\rangle\|v\|^{-2}v\|^2\) gives
\(0\leq\|u\|^2-|\langle u,v\rangle|^2/\|v\|^2\), proving
Cauchy--Schwarz. Then
\[\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2
\leq(\|u\|+\|v\|)^2,\]
and taking nonnegative square roots proves the triangle inequality.
\end{proof}

\section{Orthogonality and Gram--Schmidt}
Orthogonality makes coordinate calculations unusually simple: cross terms
vanish. Gram--Schmidt converts any basis into one with this property while
preserving the subspaces spanned at each stage.

\begin{definition}
Vectors \(u,v\) are \emph{orthogonal}, written \(u\perp v\), if
\(\langle u,v\rangle=0\). A set is \emph{orthogonal} if distinct members are
orthogonal, and \emph{orthonormal} if in addition every member has norm one.
For \(S\subseteq V\), set
\[S^\perp=\{v\in V:\langle v,s\rangle=0\text{ for every }s\in S\}.\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Gram--Schmidt]
If \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) is linearly independent, there is an orthonormal list
\(e_1,\ldots,e_n\) such that
\[\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_j)=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_j)
\quad(1\leq j\leq n).\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Set \(u_1=v_1\), \(e_1=u_1/\|u_1\|\). Given \(e_1,\ldots,e_{j-1}\), define
\[u_j=v_j-\sum_{i<j}\langle v_j,e_i\rangle e_i.\]
The convention on linearity makes
\(\langle u_j,e_k\rangle=\langle v_j,e_k\rangle-
\langle v_j,e_k\rangle=0\). If \(u_j=0\), then \(v_j\) is in the span of
earlier vectors, contrary to independence. Thus \(e_j=u_j/\|u_j\|\) is
defined. The formula also shows that replacing \(v_j\) by \(u_j\) preserves
the span at each stage.
\end{proof}

Every finite-dimensional inner-product space has a finite basis by
Chapter~6, so Gram--Schmidt applied to that basis gives an orthonormal basis.
For an orthonormal basis, taking the inner product of
\[
v=\sum_i a_ie_i
\]
with \(e_j\) proves \(a_j=\langle v,e_j\rangle\); hence the expansion is
\[
v=\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i.
\]

\begin{example}[Gram--Schmidt in \(\mathbb R^2\)]
Starting with \(v_1=(1,1)\), \(v_2=(1,0)\), one gets
\[e_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1),\qquad
u_2=(1,0)-\left\langle(1,0),e_1\right\rangle e_1
=\left(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right).
\]
Normalizing gives \(e_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\). The original vectors and
the orthonormal vectors span the same plane, but the latter make lengths and
orthogonal projections immediate.
\end{example}

\section{Orthogonal Projections}
The complement of a subspace need not be geometrically meaningful, but its
orthogonal complement is canonical. It separates a vector into its component
inside the subspace and its perpendicular error.

\begin{theorem}[Orthogonal decomposition]
For a subspace \(W\leq V\),
\[V=W\oplus W^\perp.\]
If \(e_1,\ldots,e_r\) is an orthonormal basis of \(W\), then
\[P_W(v)=\sum_{i=1}^r\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i\]
lies in \(W\), and \(v-P_W(v)\in W^\perp\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The displayed formula lies in \(W\). Its difference from \(v\) has inner
product zero with every \(e_i\), hence with every vector of \(W\). Thus
\(V=W+W^\perp\). A vector in the intersection has norm squared zero, so the
sum is direct. The stated map \(P_W\) is the \emph{orthogonal projection}.
\end{proof}

Geometrically, \(P_W(v)\) is the closest point of \(W\) to \(v\): for
\(w\in W\), orthogonality gives
\(\|v-w\|^2=\|v-P_Wv\|^2+\|P_Wv-w\|^2\).

\begin{example}[Projection onto a line]
For \(W=\operatorname{span}(1,1,1)\subseteq\mathbb R^3\), the unit vector
is \(e=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3\). Hence
\[P_W(1,2,3)=\langle(1,2,3),e\rangle e=2(1,1,1).\]
The difference \((-1,0,1)\) has dot product zero with \((1,1,1)\), visibly
confirming the decomposition into parallel and perpendicular components.
\end{example}

\section{Adjoints}
In ordinary coordinates, transpose and conjugate transpose reverse a linear
map with respect to the dot product. The adjoint is the basis-free version of
that reversal.

\begin{theorem}[Adjoint]
For every linear map \(T:V\to W\), there is a unique linear map
\(T^*:W\to V\) satisfying
\[\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle\qquad(v\in V,w\in W).\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose orthonormal bases of \(V\) and \(W\), and let \(A\) be the matrix of
\(T\). Define \(T^*\) to have matrix \(A^*=\overline{A}^{\,t}\). For
coordinate columns \(x,y\),
\[\langle Ax,y\rangle=(Ax)^t\overline y
=x^tA^t\overline y
=x^t\overline{(\overline A^{\,t}y)}
=\langle x,\overline A^{\,t}y\rangle.
\]
This gives the displayed equality. If \(S\) also has the
property, then \(\langle v,(S-T^*)w\rangle=0\) for every \(v\); taking
\(v=(S-T^*)w\) proves \(S=T^*\). Linearity follows directly from the
defining identity and uniqueness.
\end{proof}

Thus, in orthonormal bases, adjoint means transpose over \(\mathbb R\) and
conjugate transpose over \(\mathbb C\). The identities
\((ST)^*=T^*S^*\) and \((T^*)^*=T\) follow by uniqueness.

\begin{example}[A concrete adjoint]
For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) on \(\mathbb C^2\),
\[A^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-i&2\end{pmatrix}.\]
Thus the adjoint is not merely a transpose: conjugation changes the sign of
\(i\). This is why Hermitian matrices are the complex analogue of real
symmetric matrices.
\end{example}

\section{Important Classes of Operators}
The adjoint distinguishes operators compatible with the geometry from arbitrary
ones. These are exactly the operators for which diagonalization can often be
made orthonormal.

\begin{definition}
An endomorphism \(T\) is \emph{self-adjoint} (Hermitian over \(\mathbb C\))
if \(T=T^*\), \emph{skew-adjoint} if \(T^*=-T\), and \emph{normal} if
\(TT^*=T^*T\). It is \emph{unitary} over \(\mathbb C\), or \emph{orthogonal}
over \(\mathbb R\), if \(T^*T=I\). It is \emph{positive} if it is
self-adjoint and \(\langle Tv,v\rangle\geq0\) for all \(v\).
\end{definition}

Self-adjoint matrices are real symmetric or complex Hermitian. Unitary and
orthogonal maps preserve inner products and hence norms, since
\(\langle Tu,Tv\rangle=\langle u,T^*Tv\rangle\). Every \(T^*T\) is
self-adjoint and positive, because
\(\langle T^*Tv,v\rangle=\langle Tv,Tv\rangle\geq0\).

\begin{example}[Relations that should not be conflated]
Every unitary operator is normal: \(T^*T=I\) makes \(T\) injective, hence
bijective in finite dimensions, and so \(T^*=T^{-1}\) also gives \(TT^*=I\).
A self-adjoint operator is normal as well. The converses fail: multiplication
by \(i\) on \(\mathbb C\) is unitary and normal but not self-adjoint, while
the positive matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) is self-adjoint
but not unitary. These distinctions explain why normality, rather than
self-adjointness alone, is the right complex hypothesis for the full spectral
theorem.
\end{example}

\section{Spectral Theorem}
The spectral theorem identifies the precise additional condition under which
ordinary diagonalization can be made geometric.

\begin{unremark}[The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra]
The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra says that every nonconstant polynomial in
\(\mathbb C[x]\) has a root in \(\mathbb C\).  We use it here only to ensure
that the characteristic polynomial of a complex endomorphism has a root, hence
that the operator has an eigenvalue.  Classically this theorem is proved by
complex analysis, for example using Liouville's theorem or the
maximum-modulus principle; no complex analysis is developed here.  A different
Galois-theoretic proof appears later in Chapter~12.
\end{unremark}

\begin{lemma}
If \(T\) is normal on a complex inner-product space and \(Tv=\lambda v\),
then \(T^*v=\overline\lambda v\). Consequently \(\langle v,w\rangle=0\)
for eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The operator \(T-\lambda I\) is normal, and normality gives
\(\|(T-\lambda I)z\|=\|(T^*-\overline\lambda I)z\|\) for every \(z\):
the squares of these norms are equal because the operator commutes with its
adjoint. Taking \(z=v\), the left side is zero, proving the first claim.
If \(Tw=\mu w\), then
\[\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=\langle Tv,w\rangle
=\langle v,T^*w\rangle
=\langle v,\overline\mu w\rangle=\mu\langle v,w\rangle.\]
Thus \((\lambda-\mu)\langle v,w\rangle=0\), proving orthogonality when
the eigenvalues are distinct.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Complex spectral theorem]
For an endomorphism \(T\) of a finite-dimensional complex inner-product
space, \(T\) is normal if and only if it has an orthonormal basis of
eigenvectors. Equivalently, it is unitarily diagonalizable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If an orthonormal eigenbasis exists, the matrix of \(T\) is diagonal and
commutes with its conjugate transpose, so \(T\) is normal. Conversely,
the fundamental theorem of algebra supplies an eigenvalue of a normal \(T\).
Let \(E\) be its eigenspace. If \(z\in E^\perp\) and \(e\in E\), then
\[\langle Tz,e\rangle=\langle z,T^*e\rangle
=\langle z,\overline\lambda e\rangle=\lambda\langle z,e\rangle=0.\]
Thus \(E^\perp\) is \(T\)-invariant; the same calculation with \(T^*\)
shows that it is also \(T^*\)-invariant. The restriction of \(T\) to
\(E^\perp\) remains normal because both restricted products agree. Its
dimension is smaller unless \(E=V\), so induction on dimension supplies an
orthonormal eigenbasis there. Gram--Schmidt supplies an orthonormal basis of
the finite-dimensional subspace \(E\); combining the two bases proves the
claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Real spectral theorem]
A real symmetric operator has an orthonormal basis of real eigenvectors and
is orthogonally diagonalizable.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Complexify the operator. It is Hermitian, hence unitarily diagonalizable.
Its eigenvalues are real because \(\lambda\langle v,v\rangle
=\langle Tv,v\rangle=\langle v,Tv\rangle=\overline\lambda\langle v,v\rangle\).
For a real eigenvalue, the real and imaginary parts of a complex eigenvector
are real eigenvectors. More precisely,
\[
E_\lambda(T_{\mathbb C})
=\{x+iy:x,y\in E_\lambda(T)\},
\]
because \(T(x+iy)=\lambda(x+iy)\) implies \(Tx=\lambda x\) and
\(Ty=\lambda y\). Since the complex eigenspaces span the complexification,
the corresponding real eigenspaces span \(V\). Applying Gram--Schmidt within
the mutually orthogonal real eigenspaces produces a real orthonormal
eigenbasis.
\end{proof}

Ordinary diagonalization asks for some eigenbasis. Unitary or orthogonal
diagonalization asks for an orthonormal eigenbasis, a strictly stronger demand.

\begin{example}[Normal diagonalization]
The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}\) is normal and is already
unitarily diagonal. By contrast,
the family \(\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) is diagonalizable if
and only if \(a=0\). For \(a\ne0\), it is not normal, since it does not commute with
its conjugate transpose. The spectral theorem therefore correctly excludes
the Jordan-block obstruction seen in Chapter~6.
\end{example}

\section{Singular Value Decomposition}
Eigenvalues concern endomorphisms, while applications often involve a map
between spaces of different dimensions. SVD answers this broader problem by
diagonalizing the positive operator \(T^*T\).

\begin{theorem}[Singular value decomposition]
Let \(T:V\to W\) be a linear map of finite-dimensional real or complex
inner-product spaces. There are orthonormal bases of \(V\) and \(W\) for
which the matrix of \(T\) is diagonal rectangular with nonnegative entries
\(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r\). Thus, for matrices,
\[A=U\Sigma V^*\]
with \(U,V\) unitary (orthogonal in the real case). The nonzero \(\sigma_i\)
are the square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of \(T^*T\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The operator \(T^*T\) is self-adjoint and positive. By the spectral theorem,
choose an orthonormal eigenbasis \(v_i\), with eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\geq0\).
For each positive \(\sigma_i\), set \(u_i=Tv_i/\sigma_i\). Then
\[
\langle u_i,u_j\rangle
=\frac{\langle T^*Tv_i,v_j\rangle}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}
=\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}\delta_{ij}
=\delta_{ij}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\ker(T^*T)=\ker T.
\]
Indeed,
\[
\langle T^*Tv,v\rangle=\langle Tv,Tv\rangle=\lVert Tv\rVert^2.
\]
Thus \(T^*Tv=0\) implies \(Tv=0\), and the reverse implication is immediate.
The remaining \(v_i\)'s, those with eigenvalue \(0\), therefore form a basis
of \(\ker T\), and
the \(u_i\)'s extend to an orthonormal basis of \(W\). In these bases,
\(Tv_i=\sigma_i u_i\) for positive singular values and \(Tv_i=0\) on the
kernel, which is exactly the displayed diagonal rectangular matrix.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[An SVD calculation]
For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\),
\[A^*A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its orthonormal eigenvectors are
\(v_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2\), with eigenvalue \(2\), and
\(v_2=(1,-1)/\sqrt2\), with eigenvalue \(0\). Thus the singular values are
\(\sqrt2,0\). Taking \(u_1=Av_1/\sqrt2=(1,0)\) and completing it by
\(u_2=(0,1)\) gives \(A=U\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt2,0)V^*\), where \(U=I\)
and \(V\) has columns \(v_1,v_2\). Unlike eigenvalue diagonalization, this
description works even though the original matrix is not normal.
\end{example}

\section*{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}[Basic] Verify that the standard formula on \(\mathbb C^n\)
is an inner product under the convention of this chapter.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Core] Apply Gram--Schmidt to \((1,1,0),(1,0,1),(0,1,1)\)
in \(\mathbb R^3\).\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Core] Find the orthogonal projection of \((1,2,3)\) onto
the span of \((1,1,1)\).\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Core] Compute the adjoint of
\(\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) on \(\mathbb C^2\).\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Further] Show that every unitary operator is normal and all
of its eigenvalues have absolute value one.\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Further, Challenging] Find an SVD of
\(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\).\end{exercise}
